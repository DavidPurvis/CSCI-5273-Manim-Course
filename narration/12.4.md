# Dish 12.4: Money shapes paths

## 12.4.question
Networks are businesses. Carrying traffic costs money, and some of it pays and some doesn't. If you ran a network, whose traffic would you carry for free? Whose routes would you repeat? Those two business questions end up explaining most of Internet routing.

## 12.4.rel
There are two main relationships. In a customer-provider link, the customer pays the provider for connectivity to the rest of the Internet. We draw the arrow pointing from customer up to provider. In peering, two networks exchange traffic, typically without payment, but only for themselves and their customers. A peer isn't offering you transit to the world. It's only offering you what's below it.

## 12.4.export
From these contracts comes one question that decides every export. Would this make me carry traffic between two networks, when neither is my customer? If a route came from a customer, or it's my own, then I tell everyone, because traffic toward it earns me money. If it came from a peer or a provider, I tell only my customers. Telling a provider about my peer's route would invite my provider to send traffic through me, to my peer, for free, while I pay for it. So read the table. Own and customer routes go everywhere. Peer and provider routes go only to customers.

## 12.4.tiers
Those contracts stack up into a structure. At the top are Tier 1 providers, with no upstream provider at all, connected to each other as peers. Below them are other providers, which sell transit to customers, but must buy it from someone. At the bottom are stub ASes, which provide no transit, and those are the vast majority. And this graph has a key property. It's directed and acyclic. If u is a customer of v, and v is a customer of w, then w isn't a customer of u. Remember that when we come to stability.

## 12.4.valley
Those exports have a visible consequence in the data path. A permitted path climbs up through customer to provider links, crosses at most one peer link, and then descends through provider to customer links. It's up, peer, down. It's called valley free. Going down and then back up would be a valley: some customer would be carrying traffic between its two providers, paying both for the privilege. Nobody signs up for that.

## 12.4.classify
Classify the instructor's paths, reading in the direction data travels. 1, 2, d: down, down. Valid. 7, d: up. Valid. 5, 8, d: 8 is 5's customer, so that's down, then up. A valley. Invalid, since 8 would carry traffic between its providers. 6, 4, 3, d: up, peer, down. Valid. 8, 5, d: up, peer. Valid. 6, 5, d: peer, peer. Invalid, because a path can have only one peer crossing. And 1, 4, 3, d: down, peer, down. Invalid, because the peer crossing comes after descending.

## 12.4.reverse
Look at two of those again. 5, 8, d is invalid. And 8, 5, d is valid. They use nearly the same networks, in a different direction. So direction matters, and the set of nodes alone tells you nothing. Build the relationship sequence, and read it, rather than memorizing node numbers.

## 12.4.gao
The payoff, connecting to the oscillating ring from last chapter. Gao and Rexford showed that three commercial habits are enough for stability, under their model. One: the customer-provider hierarchy has no cycles, so nobody is their own provider's provider. Two: prefer customer routes over peer and provider routes. Three: follow the export rule we just saw. Why does the bad gadget disappear?

## 12.4.gao-2
Its cycle needs each network to prefer a route through its neighbor, around a ring. Under these rules, that ring of preferences can't form. Those are sufficient conditions in the model. They aren't a claim that every BGP deployment follows them, or that every stable network must. Still, it's striking. No one coordinates globally. Each business pursues its own interest, and the system tends to settle.

## 12.4.laptop
Look at the whole path of our laptop's packet. It climbs from the laptop's provider, perhaps crosses one peer link, and descends to the server's provider. Contracts set that shape. The shortest path on a map had very little to do with it.

## 12.4.pitfall
A short path in the drawing isn't necessarily a usable one. If export policy never advertised it, no traffic will follow it, however connected it looks.

## 12.4.changed
A business decision. AS A has a peer P, a provider U, and a customer C. It learns a route from P. And it learns another route from its customer C.

## 12.4.predict
Who hears about P's route from A? Who hears about C's route?

## 12.4.answer
P's route goes only to customer C. Telling U would have A carrying traffic between its provider and its peer. C's route goes to everyone: U, P, and C itself has it already. That's exactly the reachability A is being paid to spread. So far, every packet has had one destination. But what if one address should mean several servers? Or what if a thousand receivers all want the same stream? Our next chapter changes who a destination is.
