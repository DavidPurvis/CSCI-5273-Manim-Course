# Dish 12.1: What BGP carries

## 12.1.question
Our laptop's packet to the web server has so far crossed two friendly routers. On the real Internet, it would cross networks owned by companies that compete with each other, each one keeping its internal design private, and each one with its own idea of what a good route means. How can those rivals still cooperate well enough to deliver a packet? There's exactly one protocol doing this job across the whole planet, and we need to see what it actually says.

## 12.1.laptop
Our laptop's server sits in a different network from the laptop, run by a different company. Everything we've built so far works inside one organization. So who tells R2's network that the server's prefix even exists, and why would they agree to carry it?

## 12.1.history
A little context. The Internet's interdomain routing protocol is BGP, and it's been in use, in revised versions, since the early days of the commercial Internet. It's a path-vector protocol, the very idea from chapter 6. Now we can fill in how it really works.

## 12.1.as
The unit of cooperation is the autonomous system, or AS. That's a set of networks under one routing administration, identified by a number. A university, an Internet provider, or a content company can each be an AS. Inside, it may run many routers, using OSPF or IS-IS, and that's nobody else's business. From the outside, we draw it as a single cloud. This is an abstraction with a price, which we'll get to in a moment.

## 12.1.session
BGP, the Border Gateway Protocol, is what those clouds speak to each other. Two routers open a TCP connection on port 179, called a session. First they exchange all their active routes. After that they send only changes: announcements of new routes, and withdrawals of old ones. That makes it an incremental protocol. It's also prefix based, so what it advertises is reachability to a block of addresses, like 203.0.113.0/24, not to individual machines.

## 12.1.route
What exactly is a BGP route? It's a prefix, plus attributes. Two matter most right now. AS PATH is the list of autonomous systems the route has passed through. NEXT HOP says which IP address to send toward. Watch how the path grows. AS 300 owns the prefix, and it tells AS 200 the path is just 300. AS 200 adds itself, and tells AS 100 that the path is 200, 300.

## 12.1.loopcheck
Inside that list is our loop check from before. A receiving AS looks for its own number, and if it's there, the route is rejected. That handles one kind of consistency. We've already seen that it doesn't prevent path exploration or policy oscillation.

## 12.1.abstraction
The price of the abstraction. Is a shorter AS path a shorter route? Not necessarily. Here are two options. One crosses 2 ASes, but each contains a long chain of routers. The other crosses 3 ASes, each with only one or two routers. Measured in router hops, the 3 AS path is the shorter of the two. And it says nothing at all about delay. AS path length counts organizations, not distance.

## 12.1.pitfall
Don't read too much into AS path length. It counts networks, not routers. It says nothing about latency or capacity, and prepending can make it artificial.

## 12.1.changed
You try. AS 200 gets an offer of 300, 500. It passes that along to AS 100, and AS 500 is also a neighbor of AS 100. Suppose AS 100 then re-advertises its path to AS 500.

## 12.1.predict
What is the path list when it reaches AS 500? And what will AS 500 do with it?

## 12.1.answer
AS 500 receives 100, 200, 300, 500. It sees its own number at the end, and it rejects the route. Notice one thing we still haven't said: when AS 100 has several acceptable routes, how does it pick? And who decides which neighbors hear about the choice? That isn't physics, and it isn't shortest paths. It's policy.
