# Dish 15.2: Radio stack and tunnels

## 15.2.question
A phone's IP packet has to cross a noisy radio link, then a carrier's core network, and every few minutes the radio end of that path moves. How do you carry a packet through all that, so the packet itself never needs to know?

## 15.2.stack
Start with the radio hop. LTE splits its link layer into pieces. PDCP handles header compression and encryption, so scarce radio bits aren't wasted on bulky headers. RLC handles fragmentation and reassembly, and reliable transfer over the radio. Below that sits the medium access layer. Note that RLC segmentation is not IPv4 fragmentation. The headers are different, and so is the scope of reassembly. The radio link fixes its own problems locally.

## 15.2.sched
How do users share the radio? Unlike Wi-Fi, there's no contention for the downlink. The base station is a scheduler. It uses OFDM, which divides the channel into many orthogonal frequency subcarriers. Picture a grid, with time along one axis, and frequency along the other. The scheduler hands each small block to one user. A user with good conditions may get more. That grid only shows opportunities, and doesn't give you application throughput, because coding, modulation, and overhead still apply.

## 15.2.gtp
The core. Inside the core, the phone's packet isn't routed by its own address. It travels in a tunnel, using GTP, the GPRS tunneling protocol. The eNodeB wraps the phone's IP packet in a GTP header, that in UDP, and that in an outer IP header addressed to the S-GW. Core routers read only that outer header. The S-GW then unwraps and rewraps, into a new tunnel toward the P-GW. There the outer layers come off, and the original packet heads into the Internet, unchanged.

## 15.2.why
What's the benefit of all that wrapping? The radio end of the tunnel can change, without every Internet router learning a new route for this phone. The inner address stays fixed, while only the outer destination moves. And the core needs nothing beyond plain IP routing.

## 15.2.attach
How does a phone get started? It first synchronizes with a nearby cell, and reads broadcast system information. It selects a base station. Then control setup, with authentication through the MME and HSS. And finally the data path is built. Finding a signal doesn't prove that a tunnel, or even an authorized subscription, is ready. Those are distinct stages.

## 15.2.sleep
Radios drain batteries, so an idle phone sleeps. It can go into a deeper sleep, where it no longer tracks a specific cell, and then the network knows it only roughly. To reach it, the network pages it across that area. When it wakes, it might have moved, and may need a new association. It's the same trade-off as Wi-Fi sleep: battery against delivery, with cellular machinery.

## 15.2.sleep-more
Follow one phone. It sleeps, moves into another cell, and then becomes active. The old radio path can't simply be assumed, so it may need to reestablish access through the new cell before traffic can resume.

## 15.2.interconnect
One more view. Carriers are networks of IP networks, joined by interconnections, just like the rest of the Internet. Your phone might attach in a visited network, while your subscriber record stays at home, in your home carrier's HSS. So every topic in this course reappears here: tunnels, routers, and policy agreements between providers.

## 15.2.fiveg
5G New Radio extends the physical layer. Higher frequencies offer more capacity, but shorter range, so cells must be much denser. MIMO uses many antennas to send and receive multiple spatial streams. The slide's specific rates and goals describe the era of those slides, so treat them as context. The trade-off is what lasts: more spectrum and antennas for capacity, paid for with deployment density.

## 15.2.pitfall
Don't mix these up. CDMA separates senders by code. OFDM organizes the channel by frequency and time, and the scheduler hands out those resources.

## 15.2.changed
Look at one packet, deep in the core, between S-GW and P-GW. Its inner destination is a web server on the Internet.

## 15.2.predict
Which address does a core router in the middle use to forward it? Does that router need a route to the phone?

## 15.2.answer
It reads the outer destination, the P-GW. It needs no route to the phone at all. That's the whole benefit: a carrier's core routers know nothing about individual subscribers. And now the big question. Your phone has moved between cities, or even to another carrier's network. Its identity hasn't changed, but where it sits has. How does anybody find it?
