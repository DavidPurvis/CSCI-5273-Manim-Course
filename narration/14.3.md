# Dish 14.3: Joining and moving

## 14.3.question
Open your laptop in a coffee shop. Several networks appear, and you click one. What has actually happened? Are you on the Internet yet? And when the frame leaves the laptop, whose address goes on it, the access point's, or the router's?

## 14.3.channels
Start with the vocabulary. An access point and the stations associated with it form a basic service set, or BSS. Wi-Fi divides its spectrum into channels, and each access point operates on a chosen channel. Neighboring access points on overlapping channels interfere. The SSID is the human-readable network name, and the BSSID identifies one particular BSS. None of these are subnet masks, or IP addresses.

## 14.3.scan
How do you find one? Two ways. In passive scanning, access points regularly send out beacons, and the laptop just listens. In active scanning, the laptop sends a probe request, and access points answer with probe responses. Either way, you end up with a list of candidates, and you choose one. Passive scanning costs the laptop only listening time. Active scanning finds networks sooner, at the price of a few transmissions.

## 14.3.assoc
That final exchange, a request and a reply, is association. It creates a link-layer relationship with one access point. It doesn't give you an IP address. So your laptop still needs DHCP, then ARP, then DNS, exactly as in chapter 5. Being associated means you can reach the AP, and nothing more.

## 14.3.addressing
The frame. An access point isn't a router. It bridges between radio and Ethernet. So a Wi-Fi frame needs to name both the immediate radio hop, and the final link-layer destination. Say host H1 sends to router R. Address one is the receiver on this radio hop, the AP. Address two is the transmitter, H1. Address three is R's MAC, which is where the AP should bridge the frame. The AP then emits an ordinary Ethernet frame, from H1 to R. H1 stays the Ethernet source. The AP never becomes the IP destination.

## 14.3.four
There's also a fourth address field. The slide says address 4 appears only in ad hoc mode, but its own table shows a bridged case. Don't memorize that note as a universal rule. Four-address operation is used for bridging and distribution scenarios.

## 14.3.roam
Move. The laptop walks from access point 1 to access point 2. Both connect to one switch, on the same subnet. It associates with AP 2. Its IP address stays valid, because the subnet hasn't changed. So who updates what? The switch does, through its ordinary self-learning. The laptop's next frame arrives on a new port, so the switch's table changes. No Internet routes change. Chapter 2's mechanism handles this mobility case for free.

## 14.3.pitfall
Keep the steps apart. Scanning finds an AP, and association attaches to it. Neither one gives you an IP address. That's still DHCP's job, afterward.

## 14.3.changed
Change the scenario. A new access point has the very same SSID, but it sits behind a different router, on a different IP subnet.

## 14.3.predict
Does the laptop keep working with its old address? Does matching SSID text guarantee anything?

## 14.3.answer
It does not keep working. The old address doesn't belong on that subnet, so return traffic gets routed to the wrong place. A matching SSID is just a matching name. What counts is subnet continuity. Crossing subnets is the true mobility problem, and we'll take that on next chapter. First, a problem that hits every Wi-Fi network: when stations share one channel, who's allowed to talk?
