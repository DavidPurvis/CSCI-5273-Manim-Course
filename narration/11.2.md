# Dish 11.2: Counting to infinity

## 11.2.question
Distance vector routers believe the numbers their neighbors tell them. When a link gets cheaper, that's wonderful. When a link fails, it's a disaster, and the reason is not obvious. Why should good news travel fast, while bad news crawls?

## 11.2.goodnews
Start with good news. A link cost drops. The router attached to it sees a better distance, tells its neighbor, who sees a better distance too, and tells the next. The improvement spreads in a couple of exchanges, and everyone stops. A real, better path exists, so every update is true.

## 11.2.setup
The bad news, on this tiny chain. Y reaches X directly, at distance 1. Z reaches X through Y, at distance 2. And there is no other route to X in this network at all. Now cut the link between X and Y. X is gone. Y knows immediately. What should it do? It looks at what its neighbors have advertised, and it sees Z saying, I can reach X at distance 2.

## 11.2.trap
The trap. Y has no way of knowing that Z's path runs through Y itself. A bare number hides that. So Y believes it. Z's 2 plus a link of 1 makes 3, and Y now routes to X through Z. Y tells Z the news: I'm at 3. Z depends on Y, so Z updates: 3 plus 1 is 4. Z tells Y. Y updates: 5. Then Z: 6.

## 11.2.meaning
Stop and see what those numbers mean. There is no path to X at all. Yet each router is confident. They point at each other, and their traffic bounces between them, while the numbers creep up, until they reach whatever the protocol treats as infinity. No new links appeared. The routers are slowly unlearning something false. This is called count to infinity. It's information feedback, not any physical growth in the network. And a cost increase can trigger the same counting, even if a real but expensive alternate exists. They just count up until the alternate wins.

## 11.2.rip
One crude fix is to make infinity small. RIP treats 16 as unreachable, leaving 1 through 15 as usable metrics. Now the counting stops at 16, soon rather than never. But any network wider than 15 hops can't be represented. And the underlying problem has not gone anywhere. We only bounded it.

## 11.2.poison
A better fix targets the actual lie. Go back to before the failure. Z's route to X goes through Y. So why would Z ever tell Y that it has a route to X? That information is useless to Y, and we've just seen that it's dangerous. Poison reverse says: if you route through a neighbor, tell that neighbor your distance is infinity. Z keeps its true route, and tells Y infinity. It can still tell its other neighbors the truth. So the advertisement is specific to each neighbor. It is not a command to delete anything.

## 11.2.poisoned
Replay the failure. X and Y lose their link. Y checks its neighbors. Z says infinity. So Y knows it has nowhere to go, and it tells Z so. Z's only route was through Y, so Z drops to infinity as well. Done, in one quick exchange, with no counting.

## 11.2.slide-good
See the counting itself with the slide's real numbers, on a slightly bigger network. X to Y costs 4, Y to Z costs 1, and X to Z costs 50. Y reaches X directly at 4, and Z goes through Y, at 5. Now suppose X to Y gets better, dropping to 1. Y's distance falls to 1, and it tells Z. Z computes 2. Z tells Y, and Y stays at 1. Done, in two exchanges.

## 11.2.slide-bad
Make it worse, raising X to Y from 4 to 60. Y compares a direct cost of 60 with Z's old claim of 5, through a link of 1, for a total of 6. It picks 6, through Z, not knowing that Z's 5 ran through Y. Z hears 6, and computes 7. Y hears 7, and computes 8.

## 11.2.slide-bad-2
They climb 2 at a time, while packets for X bounce between them. It only ends when going through Y costs more than Z's real direct link of 50. Then Z settles at 50, and Y at 51. That took more than twenty exchanges, for one changed number. Without the 50 link, it would never stop on its own.

## 11.2.pitfall
Making infinity small feels like a fix. It isn't. It only bounds the counting, and it limits how wide the network can be. The underlying dependency is still there.

## 11.2.changed
Is the problem solved? Add a third router to the loop. Now Y, Z, and W form a triangle, and they all reach X through Y. Z and W are each other's neighbors.

## 11.2.predict
Z poisons its route toward Y. But what does Z tell W? Could W now give Y bad advice?

## 11.2.answer
Z tells W the truth: distance 2. It doesn't route through W, so it has no reason to poison. W then thinks it has a path to X through Z, and it can tell Y. So a loop of three can still count up. Poison reverse handles the simple two-node case, not every cycle. What's the real issue? The number hides the path. So what if routers just sent the path? That's the fix BGP uses, and we'll see that even this doesn't make the trouble go away.
