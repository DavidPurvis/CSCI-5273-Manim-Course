# Dish 12.2: Import and export

## 12.2.question
A neighbor hands you a route. Do you have to believe it? Do you have to use it? If you use it, must you tell everyone else? With BGP, the answer to all three questions is no. Those are three separate decisions, and getting them apart is the key to the whole protocol.

## 12.2.pipeline
The pipeline in every BGP router. Routes arrive from neighbors. Import policy filters them, and may change their attributes. Best-route selection picks one route per prefix for use. That route goes into forwarding. Then export policy decides, separately for each neighbor, what to advertise. Because the stages are separate, a route rejected at import never becomes a candidate. A route suppressed at export can still be the one you use yourself.

## 12.2.import
Import first. Why would you ever reject a route? Say your customer owns one prefix, and suddenly announces a route to a big video site. That's either a configuration mistake, or an attack. Believing it would pull the world's traffic into your customer's network. So on a session to a customer, a network commonly accepts only the prefixes that customer actually holds. An advertisement is data to evaluate, not an order.

## 12.2.lp
Import can also attach a value called local preference. It's a number used only inside your own AS, and higher is better. It's checked before anything else, so it directly states your own ranking. For example, you might set 100 on routes from a customer, and 90 on routes from a peer, because customers pay you, and carrying traffic to them earns money. Compare. A customer route with local preference 100 and an AS path of 4, against a peer route with local preference 90 and an AS path of 2. The customer route wins. Length is never even looked at.

## 12.2.export
The other end of the pipeline. Export policy decides what each neighbor gets to hear. Why not tell everyone everything? Because telling a neighbor about a route is an offer: send your traffic through me, and I'll carry it. Advertising a route is promising to do work. So a network can use a route itself, while deliberately keeping it from a neighbor. Using a route and offering transit are different acts.

## 12.2.prepend
Export can also change how a route looks. Suppose you have two links to the Internet, and want incoming traffic on just one of them. You can't control anybody else's decision. But you can make one option look worse. AS path prepending repeats your own number. Instead of announcing 300, you announce 300, 300, 300 over the backup link. Remote networks comparing path lengths will tend to choose the other link. Tend to, because prepending can't force anything. A remote network's local preference is checked first, and it could still ignore you.

## 12.2.rule
Keep these two tools straight. Local preference ranks routes within your own AS, and it controls your outgoing traffic. Prepending changes what others see, so it only influences your incoming traffic. One is a decision. The other is a suggestion.

## 12.2.config
Where do all these policies live? Administrators write them, per neighbor, in router configuration. That's a lot of manual work, spread over many routers and many people, and mistakes do happen. A configuration error can be visible across the entire Internet.

## 12.2.pitfall
A common mistake is to weigh these numbers against each other. There's no trade between preference and path length. Preference is compared first, and length matters only if preference ties.

## 12.2.changed
A situation. Network A prepends heavily on its link to provider P, hoping traffic will arrive by way of provider Q instead. But a far-off network, Z, assigns a high local preference to routes learned through P.

## 12.2.predict
Which way does Z's traffic arrive at A? And did the prepending do anything at all?

## 12.2.answer
It arrives through P, the path A was trying to discourage, because Z compares local preference first, and never reaches the length comparison. Prepending may still influence others who have tied preferences. So that's the outside view of an AS. But an AS isn't really one node. It's a set of routers with their own geography. How do they agree on what to do?
