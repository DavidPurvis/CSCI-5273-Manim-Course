# Dish 10.1: Dijkstra

## 10.1.question
We ended the last chapter with a cut link, and a router whose table had gone wrong. So who fills in that table? A router forwards a packet using a table it reads in nanoseconds. But something has to build that table, and rebuild it when the network changes. An administrator cannot type routes into thousands of routers by hand. So how can routers, talking only to each other, work out good paths through a network none of them can see all at once?

## 10.1.laptop
Remember our laptop's packet, sitting at router R1, headed for a web server. R1 looked up the destination, and sent it out one port. This chapter is about where that table entry came from.

## 10.1.routing-forwarding
Start by separating two words. Forwarding is what a router does to one packet, right now: read the address, look it up, and send it out. Routing is how the table got there in the first place, a network-wide process of exchanging information and computing paths. And a route to a far prefix needs two pieces of knowledge. Which router, or exit, can reach that prefix? And how do I reach that router from here? This lecture solves the second piece. BGP will supply the first. Could you just type all this in by hand? Static routes don't adapt to failures, new equipment, or load.

## 10.1.model
First we need a model. Draw the network as a graph, with routers as nodes and links as edges, each with a cost. A path's cost is the sum of its edge costs. Our goal is the cheapest path from a source to each destination. If every edge costs one, this just counts hops.

## 10.1.model-2
But weights change the answer. Here, going from A to C directly costs 5. Going through B costs 2 plus 1, which is only 3. So the path with more hops is the better one. This makes sense inside one organization, where everyone agrees what the numbers mean. We'll see later that it does not fit the whole Internet.

## 10.1.linkstate
One approach is link-state routing, and its idea has two steps. Step one: every router tells everyone about its own links. Those messages flood through the whole network, so eventually every router holds the same map. Step two: each router independently runs a shortest path computation on its copy of that map, with itself as the source. It shares facts, not conclusions. OSPF and IS-IS are the two link-state protocols you will meet in practice, both used inside a single organization.

## 10.1.ospf
One caution about that shared map. It's what the router currently believes, not a perfect live view of the network. If a link has just failed, some routers don't know yet. Hold on to that thought, because it creates a whole chapter of trouble. For now, assume every router's map is correct, and look at the computation itself, which comes from Edsger Dijkstra.

## 10.1.idea
Dijkstra's algorithm grows a region of settled nodes, whose best distances are final. It keeps a tentative distance for every other node. Then it repeats one step. Pick the unsettled node with the smallest tentative distance, and settle it. Next, relax its edges: for each neighbor v, ask whether going through this node beats the best distance we had. Why is it safe to settle that smallest node? Because weights are not negative, so any other route to it would have to leave through a node that is already at least as far. No detour could ever come back cheaper.

## 10.1.init
Run it from A. Start by settling A, at distance zero. Its neighbors get tentative distances equal to their direct costs: B is 2, and C is 5. D is not adjacent, so D is infinity.

## 10.1.settle-b
The frontier holds B at 2, C at 5, and D at infinity. The smallest is B, so settle it, and record that its predecessor is A. Relax B's edges. Through B, C costs 2 plus 1, which is 3. That beats 5, so C improves, and its predecessor becomes B. Through B, D costs 2 plus 4, which is 6, and that beats infinity.

## 10.1.settle-c
The frontier is C at 3, and D at 6. Settle C, through B. Relax its edge to D: 3 plus 2 is 5. That beats 6, so D improves again, and its predecessor changes from B to C. See how a tentative value can keep getting better until the moment it's settled, and never after that.

## 10.1.settle-d
Finally settle D, at 5, through C. Every node is settled. The green edges form a shortest path tree, rooted at A. Final distances: B is 2, C is 3, and D is 5.

## 10.1.firsthop
The part that catches people out. The computation gave us predecessors, but a forwarding table needs something else. D's predecessor is C. That is the node just before D on the path. But router A cannot hand a packet to C. It is not even the right neighbor. The path to D is A, B, C, D, so A's forwarding entry says: to reach D, send to B. In fact, every entry at A says B here. Distance, predecessor, and first hop are three different outputs. The first hop is the only one a forwarding table stores.

## 10.1.slide
Try the instructor's exercise. On this graph, find the shortest path from t to v. It's tempting to go through x, since t reaches x directly at 5. But then x to z to y to v adds 3, for a total of 8. The direct route from x to v, at 4, also gives a total of 8 by way of s and w. The best path is t, s, w, x, z, y, v, adding 2, 1, 1, 1, 1, and 1, which is 7. So the distance is 7, answer B, and the forwarding entry at t points to s, also answer B. More hops, lower cost.

## 10.1.bigger
The slides also trace a bigger graph from source A, over eight nodes. The method doesn't change. Settle B at 2, which improves D from 5 to 4, and gives E 9 and F 14. C and D then tie at 4. Either may go first, since a tie can alter a predecessor but no shortest distance.

## 10.1.bigger-2
Settling D improves E to 8, and gives G 7. Settling G offers E another route costing 8, another tie. Settling E improves H from 14 to 13. And F stays at 14, since going through H would cost 16. Check any of these by adding the edges along the final path. Keep your settled set, tentative distances, and tie policy all explicit, and it's mechanical.

## 10.1.pitfall
Don't confuse the two sets. A tentative distance can keep improving. A settled one is final, and that's only guaranteed because weights aren't negative.

## 10.1.changed
Return to our small graph, and change one number. Raise the cost of the link between B and C from 1 to 6. Everything else stays the same.

## 10.1.predict
Run Dijkstra in your head. What are the three distances now? And which neighbor does A use to reach C?

## 10.1.answer
Settle B at 2. Through B, C would now cost 8, which doesn't beat the direct 5. D becomes 6 through B. Then settle C at 5. Through C, D would cost 7, which doesn't beat 6. So the distances are 2, 5, and 6. A's first hop to B is B, to C is C itself, and to D is B. One number changed, and A's route to C changed completely. But before A could install this, the new cost had to reach it. Flooding carries the whole map to everyone. What if routers shared something much smaller instead?
