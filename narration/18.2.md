# Dish 18.2: One mask, one match, one fragment

## 18.2.open
A quick recompute, with new numbers, on your own paper. First, a mask. A host has address 192.168.37.200/21. Give the mask, the network, the broadcast address, and the usable count.

## 18.2.p1
Do it from a blank page.

## 18.2.a1
A slash 21 is 255.255.248.0. Third-byte blocks have size 8, and 37 lies in 32 through 39. So the network is 192.168.32.0, and the broadcast is 192.168.39.255. That's 11 host bits, so 2 to the 11th, minus 2, gives 2,046 usable addresses.

## 18.2.q2
A match. A router holds 192.168.0.0/16 to A, 192.168.32.0/21 to B, 192.168.36.0/24 to C, and slash 0 to D. Which output does that same address, 192.168.37.200, use?

## 18.2.p2
Check each prefix, then choose.

## 18.2.a2
It matches the slash 16, the slash 21, and the slash 0. It doesn't match the slash 24, because that covers only third byte 36. So the longest match is the slash 21: output B.

## 18.2.q3
Finally, a fragment. An 1,800 byte datagram, with a 20 byte header, crosses an MTU of 600. Give the largest nonfinal payload, the fragment count, and the offsets.

## 18.2.p3
Round down to a multiple of 8.

## 18.2.a3
600 minus 20 is 580, and 580 rounds down to 576. The data is 1,780 bytes, so the payloads are 576, 576, 576, and 52. That's four fragments. The offsets go up in steps of 72: 0, 72, 144, and 216. Only the last has its more fragments flag at 0.
