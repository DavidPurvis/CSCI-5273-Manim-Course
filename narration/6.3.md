# Dish 6.3: Learning and loops

## 6.3.question
Nobody configures a home switch. You plug in the cables and it works. Yet it sends frames out the correct port. Where did that knowledge come from? Pause on a clue: every frame carries two addresses. Only one of them says where a machine actually is right now.

## 6.3.setup
A switch with an empty table, and three hosts. A is on port 1, B is on port 2, and C is on port 3. A sends a frame to B. Watch the order of the next two steps, because they use two different addresses.

## 6.3.learn
First, the switch looks at the source address. This frame says it came from A, and it arrived on port 1. So A must be reachable through port 1. Write that down. Second, it looks up the destination, B. B is not in the table, so the switch has no idea. It floods the frame out every other port. Note what it did not learn. It cannot learn where B is just because A mentioned B.

## 6.3.reply
B answers. Its frame's source address teaches the switch that B is on port 2. And now the destination, A, is in the table, so this frame goes only out port 1. Finally, A sends to B once more. Nothing new to learn. The destination is known, and the frame goes only to port 2. C is no longer bothered.

## 6.3.aging
The rule is, learn from the source, then forward by the destination. Unknown destination, flood. Known destination on another port, forward. Known destination on the arrival port, filter. Entries also expire, so that a host which moves or disappears does not stay wrong forever. The slides call this a TTL. Be careful with that word. It is a local aging timer in the switch. It is not a hop counter carried in the Ethernet frame, and that fact is about to matter a great deal.

## 6.3.loop
Networks want spare links, in case one fails. So connect three switches in a triangle. Now send one broadcast. S1 floods it to S2 and S3. Each floods it onward, to the other. Those copies come back, and get flooded again. IP has a TTL that kills looping packets. Ethernet has no such field. The copies circulate and multiply until the network collapses. A cycle in the wiring is fatal.

## 6.3.stp
The fix is the spanning tree protocol. Keep every physical link, but let frames use only a loop-free subset. Switches exchange messages carrying three things: who they think the root is, their distance to it, and their own ID. Each begins by claiming to be the root. Hearing a lower ID, it gives up that claim.

## 6.3.stp-2
Hearing a shorter path to the root, it adopts that path. In the triangle, S1 becomes root, S2 and S3 use their direct links, and the link between them stops forwarding. Three switches, two active links, and no cycle. The slides simplify the real protocol, which also needs deterministic ties and a designated forwarder on each segment.

## 6.3.instructor
The exercise from the instructor's slides, with seven switches. The drawing gives no priorities or costs, so we must state an assumption. Treat all links as equal cost, and treat S1 through S7 as increasing bridge IDs. Then S1 is the root. Most switches connect to it directly. S3 is two hops away, through either S2 or S5, and the lower ID, S2, wins the tie. That leaves six active links for seven switches, exactly a tree.

## 6.3.pitfall
Watch out for this one. When a switch floods an unknown destination, the frame doesn't become a broadcast. Its destination address stays exactly as it was. Only the set of output ports changed.

## 6.3.changed
Use this tree. Host A hangs off S3, host B off S7, and host C off S4. A to B cannot use the short-looking route through S5, because those dashed links do not forward.

## 6.3.predict
Trace the other two paths on the tree. What route does A to C follow? And B to C?

## 6.3.answer
A to C goes S3, S2, S1, S4. B to C goes S7, S1, S4. Notice that A to B took three hops, though the physical drawing offers something shorter. Spanning tree does not promise shortest paths between hosts, and it does not minimize total cost either. It promises one thing: no loops. Different priorities could pick a different tree, so the assumptions stay attached to the answer.
