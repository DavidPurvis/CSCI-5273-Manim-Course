# Dish 10.2: Distance vector

## 10.2.question
Link state asks every router to store and process a map of the whole network. Could a router find good paths knowing almost nothing, just by asking its neighbors one simple question? How far are you from the destination?

## 10.2.idea
That's distance-vector routing. No router keeps a map. Each one keeps a list of destinations and its best known distance to each, and it tells that list only to its direct neighbors. Then each router combines what its neighbors said with the cost of reaching them. It's like asking for directions. You can't see the route. But if your neighbor says they're 3 kilometers from the station, and they are 3 kilometers from you, then you know something about your own distance.

## 10.2.compute
Compute one. A's link to B costs 3, and B says it's 3 away from Z. That's a total of 6. A's link to C costs 2, and C says 5. That's a total of 7. The minimum is 6, through B. So A's distance is 6, and its next hop is B. A doesn't know B's internal path to Z, and it doesn't need to. Now B advertises something new: its distance is 10. Through B would now cost 13. Through C still costs 7. So A switches to C, and its table changes with no map in sight.

## 10.2.formula
The rule, called the Bellman-Ford update. x is me. y is the destination. v ranges over my neighbors. My distance to y is the minimum, over all neighbors v, of my link cost to v, plus v's advertised distance to y. And the neighbor that gives the minimum becomes my next hop. Two terms, and they come from different places. The first I can measure myself. The second I'm trusting my neighbor about.

## 10.2.setup-rounds
How does a whole network find its answers? Knowledge spreads in rounds. Use our graph from before, and an idealized synchronous exercise: in each round, every router uses only the advertisements from the previous round. In round one, each router knows just itself and its direct neighbors. So A has B at 2, C at 5, and D at infinity.

## 10.2.round2
Round two. A hears B's vector from round one. B says it's 1 from C, and 4 from D. So through B, C costs 2 plus 1, which is 3. And D costs 2 plus 4, which is 6. A's table now reflects paths of up to two edges.

## 10.2.round3
Here's the subtle step. During round two, B also heard from C, and learned it can reach D for 1 plus 2, which is 3. In round three, B advertises that better number. A computes 2 plus 3, which is 5, through B. It has discovered the path A, B, C, D, without ever knowing that C was involved. The next round changes nothing, so it has converged, on the same answers Dijkstra gave.

## 10.2.real
Those neat rounds are a classroom picture. Real routers update whenever they like, with no global clock. A value from a neighbor could also be old, not a final truth. RIP and EIGRP are the named members of this family. RIP uses hop count as its metric. Don't assume EIGRP behaves identically, because its rules differ in detail.

## 10.2.real-more
Classify them. OSPF and IS-IS belong with link state. RIP and EIGRP belong with distance vector. RIP gives each link a hop cost of 1, so usable distances run from 1 to 15, and 16 means unreachable. Only RIP has that limit, as far as this lecture shows, so don't carry it over to EIGRP.

## 10.2.compare
Compare what's being trusted. Link state trusts link facts, and computes its own paths. Distance vector trusts its neighbors' computed results. Both reach shortest paths with a shared metric. But they share different knowledge, and that decides how they behave when things break. The reusable distinction is simple: a map, versus neighbors' distances.

## 10.2.robustness
The lecture adds a broad comparison, and you should treat it as a family-level teaching picture, rather than a universal benchmark. Link state sends more messages, since facts flood everywhere, and each router runs its own computation. If one router computes something wrong, that error stays mostly local. Distance vector exchanges only with its neighbors. But a wrong number is passed along as if it were a fact, so one router's error can spread through everyone's tables. Real costs depend on graph size and on protocol details.

## 10.2.pitfall
In a synchronous trace, here's where marks get lost. Every router computes from the previous round's advertisements. Mixing in values from the current round changes the answer.

## 10.2.changed
You do one. Router x has two neighbors. Its link to P costs 4, and P advertises 7 to the destination. Its link to Q costs 9, and Q advertises 1.

## 10.2.predict
Which neighbor wins? Then suppose P's link cost rises to 6. Does your answer change?

## 10.2.answer
P gives 11, and Q gives 10, so Q wins, even though its link is the expensive one. Raise P's link to 6, and P only gets worse, at 13, so Q still wins. Notice that the router is relying on a number somebody else told it. It has no way to know how that number was produced, and no way to know whether the path behind it runs right back through itself.
