# Dish 14.1: What radio does to bits

## 14.1.question
Our laptop leaves the wire. Everything above the link layer still works, in principle. Yet on radio, a frame that was perfect at the sender may be garbage at the receiver, and the sender may have no idea. What actually changes when the wire goes away?

## 14.1.two
First, separate two ideas people often mix up. Wireless is about the medium: communicating over a radio link. Mobility is about changing your point of attachment. A fixed sensor is wireless, without being mobile. A laptop moved between wired subnets is mobile, without being wireless. This chapter is about the radio problem. The next is about movement.

## 14.1.types
Networks also differ in structure. In infrastructure mode, hosts talk through a base station, such as a Wi-Fi access point or a cell tower, that connects into the wired network. In ad hoc mode, there's no base station, and the nodes organize themselves. And either kind can be single hop or multiple hops, as in a mesh network, where traffic is relayed through other wireless nodes. Two independent questions: is there infrastructure, and how many wireless hops?

## 14.1.rates
The slides chart radio technologies by rate and range, from Bluetooth at a couple of megabits per second, up through Wi-Fi and cellular generations. Those numbers belong to that era, and aren't promises about today's products. What lasts is the pattern. Higher rates tend to mean shorter range, and every link faces the same questions about frequency, coverage, and sharing.

## 14.1.impair
The channel. Three separate things degrade a radio signal. Path loss: the signal weakens with distance, and when passing through obstacles. Interference: other transmitters and devices add competing energy, since many of them share frequencies like 2 point 4 gigahertz. And multipath: the signal bounces off walls and the ground, so delayed copies arrive at different times, and overlap with each other. An application may see the same symptom from all three, a lost packet, but they aren't the same mechanism.

## 14.1.snr
To measure signal quality, we compare signal power to noise power. That's the signal to noise ratio, in decibels: 10 times log base 10 of signal over noise. A power ratio of 100 is 20 decibels. Higher SNR means fewer errors. The result is called bit error rate, or BER: the fraction of bits received wrong. These curves show the tradeoff. At the same SNR, a low-rate, robust scheme gives very few errors, while a high-rate scheme fails.

## 14.1.adapt
A radio link can't just pick one rate and stick to it. As a host moves away, SNR falls, and errors climb. Rate adaptation lowers the physical rate, moving to a more robust scheme. Raw rate is not goodput. Repeated failures at a high rate can deliver less than steady success at a low rate. And why not just raise the power? That burns energy, and it interferes with everybody else.

## 14.1.rule
The rule is, the link's capacity isn't a fixed number. It depends on distance, surroundings, and everyone else who's transmitting at this moment.

## 14.1.pitfall
Turning up the power seems like an easy fix for a weak signal. But it drains the battery, and it raises interference for everyone nearby.

## 14.1.changed
Try the arithmetic. A receiver's signal is 1,000 times stronger than the noise. Then the laptop walks behind a wall, and the ratio drops to 10.

## 14.1.predict
What are those two values, in decibels? And what should the sender do after the drop?

## 14.1.answer
1,000 is 30 decibels. 10 is 10 decibels. That's a large drop, and the sender should adapt to a slower, more robust rate. Each station can react to its own radio conditions.
