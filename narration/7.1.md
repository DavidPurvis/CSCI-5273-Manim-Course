# Dish 7.1: What the network promises

## 7.1.question
Our laptop's frame has reached the edge of its LAN. Beyond that lie thousands of networks, built by different owners, from different hardware. What should the network as a whole promise to deliver? A guaranteed rate? Guaranteed order? Or almost nothing at all? The surprising answer that built the Internet is: almost nothing.

## 7.1.circuit
One design comes from the telephone network: circuit switching. Before any data moves, set up a path, and reserve capacity at each hop. Once it is established, the bandwidth is yours. Quality is predictable. But if you pause, nobody else may use your reserved share, and every switch has to remember your call.

## 7.1.circuit-more
Put numbers on the waste. A 1 megabit per second circuit reserved for ten seconds allocates up to 10 megabits. If the application sends only 2, the rest of that opportunity can't necessarily be used by anyone else.

## 7.1.packet
The other design is packet switching. Chop data into packets. Give each one its own destination address, and let each hop forward it when capacity becomes available. No setup, and no reservation. No router has to remember that you exist.

## 7.1.statmux
Why does this suit computers? Their traffic is bursty. A web page arrives in a rush, and then the link goes silent. Sharing capacity according to actual arrivals is called statistical multiplexing, and it lets many bursty users share a link that could never hold all their peaks. The price comes when bursts overlap. If 8 megabits per second arrive at a 4 megabit link for 10 milliseconds, a backlog of 5,000 bytes builds in a queue.

## 7.1.best
IP makes a modest deal called best-effort service. It will try. It may lose your packet, reorder it, delay it, or even duplicate it, and none of that breaks the contract. That sounds terrible, until you see what it buys. A network that promises so little can run over nearly any link, and needs no per-flow memory inside. Reliability can then be added at the end machines, by the programs that actually need it.

## 7.1.services
Best effort is not the only possible deal. A QoS service model is a list of promises about bandwidth, loss, order, and timing. ATM's constant bit rate service reserves a fixed rate, with order and timing protected. ATM's available bit rate promises only a minimum rate. Integrated Services aims at explicit guarantees per flow. Differentiated Services treats classes of packets differently, without per-flow guarantees. Keep these promises separate. A minimum rate is not a delay bound, and priority is not an end to end guarantee.

## 7.1.planes
If routers remember nothing about flows, what do they keep? A forwarding table. And that exposes the most useful split in this course. The data plane handles every packet, using the installed table, in nanoseconds. The control plane exchanges information, and decides what belongs in that table, over seconds. The management plane is where people configure intent. Say an operator changes a link weight. A protocol recomputes. The router installs a new entry. Later packets follow it. Those are separate events, and that separation will matter when failures strike.

## 7.1.rule
Traditionally, each router runs its own control software, and they cooperate. Software-defined networking moves that logic into a controller, which programs the forwarding devices. Does every packet then have to visit the controller? No. The controller installs rules, and the device applies them locally. Either way, the rule stays the same. Control decides. Data executes.

## 7.1.pitfall
An easy mistake: if average load is below capacity, nothing should ever wait. But traffic comes in bursts, so queues still build for short moments.

## 7.1.changed
Test yourself on the contract. A packet has a valid address. Its checksum is perfect, and a route exists. It still never arrives.

## 7.1.predict
Did the network break its promise? Name two ways this could happen.

## 7.1.answer
No promise was broken. A full queue may drop it. Its TTL may expire. A failure or a policy may stop it. Best effort means exactly that. So what does the network truly require from each packet? An address that can guide it across thousands of networks, without each router knowing every host. That is our next problem.
