# Dish 7.4: Fragmentation

## 7.4.question
IP runs over any link, and links limit how big a packet they will carry. That limit is the MTU. What happens when a 4,000 byte datagram reaches a link with an MTU of only 1,500 bytes?

## 7.4.idea
If fragmentation is allowed, the router splits it into smaller datagrams. Each fragment gets its own full IP header, so each travels independently. They all keep the original's identification number, so the destination can tell which ones belong together. Reassembly normally happens only at the destination.

## 7.4.payload
The arithmetic, carefully. Total length is 4,000, and 20 bytes of that is header. So the data to carry is 3,980 bytes. Each fragment on the small link can hold 1,500 minus 20, which is 1,480 bytes of data. And nonfinal fragments must carry a multiple of 8 bytes, because the offset field counts in 8 byte units. 1,480 divides evenly by 8. So 1,480 it is.

## 7.4.table
The first fragment carries bytes 0 through 1,479, offset 0, more fragments flag set. The second carries the next 1,480. Its offset is 1,480 divided by 8, which is 185. The flag is still set. The third carries what remains, 1,020 bytes. Its offset is 2,960 divided by 8, which is 370, and its more fragments flag is 0, marking the end.

## 7.4.check
Check it. The payloads sum to 3,980, which is the original data, restored exactly. But the total bytes sent are 4,040, not 4,000, because there are now three headers instead of one. Fragmentation costs overhead, and if any one fragment is lost, the whole datagram is lost.

## 7.4.rule
Keep the rule short. Offset is the data position in the original, divided by 8. It is not the fragment's length, and it does not count headers. Fragmentation exists to fit a link, and it has nothing to do with repairing congestion loss.

## 7.4.pitfall
One more trap. A router can't always fragment. If the Don't Fragment flag is set, it isn't allowed to. And losing any one fragment loses the whole datagram.

## 7.4.changed
New numbers. Total length 3,200, header 20, MTU 1,000. This time 1,000 minus 20 is 980, and 980 is not a multiple of 8.

## 7.4.predict
What is the largest nonfinal payload? How many fragments will there be, and what are their offsets?

## 7.4.answer
Round 980 down to a multiple of 8, and you get 976. The data is 3,180 bytes, giving payloads of 976, 976, 976, and a final 252. The offsets go up in steps of 122: 0, 122, 244, 366. Those four unused bytes per fragment are the price of 8 byte alignment. We now know what a packet looks like.
