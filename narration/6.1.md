# Dish 6.1: Sharing a wire

## 6.1.question
Our laptop's very first hop crosses a local network. So start there. Suppose many computers share one wire, and whatever one sends, everybody hears. If two speak at once, the signals garble each other. Who gets to talk, and when? Notice this is not a technical detail. It is a resource allocation problem.

## 6.1.partition
One family of answers is channel partitioning. Divide the wire into fixed shares of time or frequency, one for each user. With four users on a 12 megabit per second wire, each user owns 3 megabits per second. It is perfectly fair and collision free. But watch what happens when only one user has anything to send. Three quarters of the wire sits idle, and that lone user still gets just 3.

## 6.1.turns
A second family is taking turns. A controller polls each station, or a token passes around, so only the holder may send. Idle stations can be skipped, which fixes the waste. The price is control overhead, and a dependency on the coordinator or the token.

## 6.1.random
The third family is random access. Do not coordinate at all. Whoever has data just tries. When the network is quiet, a lone sender gets nearly the whole wire. When many are active, collisions waste capacity. None of the three wins everywhere. They make different trades among idle capacity, control overhead, and contention. Ethernet chose random access.

## 6.1.frame
Before watching Ethernet contend, look at what it sends. A frame starts with a preamble, which lets the receiver's clock synchronize. Then a destination address, a source address, a type field saying what the payload is, such as IP, then the payload, and finally a CRC that detects corruption. The CRC only detects. It does not repair, and Ethernet sends no acknowledgment. Delivery here is best effort.

## 6.1.mac
Those addresses are MAC addresses, 48 bits burned into each network interface. A MAC address is flat, like a serial number. It says nothing about location, and it has meaning only for local delivery. A network card keeps a frame only if the destination is its own address, or the broadcast address. Everything else, it ignores.

## 6.1.mac-more
Picture one LAN. A host has one IP address and one MAC address, written as six hexadecimal bytes. A switch learns that MAC from incoming frames. A sending host learns the IP to MAC mapping through ARP. And the broadcast address is all ones.

## 6.1.csma
The Ethernet rule, called CSMA/CD. Carrier sense: listen first, and if someone is talking, wait. Collision detection: keep listening while you send. If what you hear differs from what you sent, there is a collision, so stop and send a brief jam signal. But why would a collision ever happen, if everyone listens first? Because signals take time to travel. A and B can both hear silence, because neither signal has arrived yet.

## 6.1.backoff
After a collision, if both retry immediately, they collide again forever. So each picks a random wait. After the m-th collision, choose K from zero up to 2 to the m, minus one, and wait K times 512 bit times. Each further collision doubles the range, so the group spreads itself out without any coordination. That is binary exponential backoff. Try one. After the third collision, K ranges from 0 to 7. On 10 megabit Ethernet, 512 bit times is 51 point 2 microseconds. So K equal to 5 means a wait of 256 microseconds.

## 6.1.window
There is one more trap. A sender can only detect a collision while it is still transmitting. Suppose A starts at time zero, and the one-way delay is d. B starts just before A's signal arrives, at almost d. B's collision news then needs another d to return. So A may not hear about it until 2 d. So A must still be sending at 2 d, which gives the rule: frame length over rate must be at least twice the propagation delay.

## 6.1.pitfall
A tempting one: if everyone listens before sending, collisions can't happen. But a signal takes time to travel, so two stations can both hear silence, and both start.

## 6.1.changed
Put in the classic numbers. At 10 megabits per second, with a one-way delay of 25 point 6 microseconds, the frame must last at least 51 point 2 microseconds. That is 512 bits, which is the famous 64 byte minimum Ethernet frame. Now change the delay to just 20 microseconds, at the same rate.

## 6.1.predict
What minimum length does the same formula give now, in bits?

## 6.1.answer
Two times 20 microseconds times 10 megabits per second is 400 bits. That is the formula's answer. It does not replace Ethernet's real minimum of 64 bytes, and the rule belongs to shared, half-duplex wires. Notice what the whole design has been fighting: everyone shares one collision domain. So the next question is obvious. Could we simply stop sharing?
