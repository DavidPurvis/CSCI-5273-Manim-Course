# Dish 14.4: Contention and reservations

## 14.4.question
Ethernet senders listen before and during transmission, and they abort on a collision. Why can't Wi-Fi do the same thing? It has a radio, it can listen. Is there something about radio that breaks collision detection?

## 14.4.why
There are two reasons. First, while transmitting, a radio's own signal overwhelms the tiny signal arriving from elsewhere, so it can't really listen while it talks. Second, and more deeply, what matters isn't what the sender hears. What matters is what the receiver hears. That difference needs a picture.

## 14.4.hidden
A, B, and C sit in a row. Each one's radio range reaches only its neighbors. A hears B. C hears B. But A and C can't hear each other. A senses the channel: silence. It starts sending to B. C senses too: silence, because it's out of A's range. It also starts sending to B. Each sender was perfectly polite, and both frames are destroyed at B. Neither of them noticed anything. This is the hidden terminal problem. Local sensing simply can't see interference at a distant receiver.

## 14.4.exposed
There's a mirror image. Now B sends to A, and C wants to send to D, on the far side. C hears B, so carrier sense says wait. But C's signal wouldn't reach A at all. These two transmissions could have happened at the same time safely. Sensing suppressed useful concurrency. That's the exposed terminal problem. A note of care, though. The slide's shortcut, about hearing an RTS but not a CTS, is an idealized thought experiment. Real timing and acknowledgments must work too, so the drawing alone isn't permission to transmit.

## 14.4.csmaca
Wi-Fi avoids collisions instead of detecting them. It's called CSMA/CA. Here's the sender's routine. Wait until the channel has been idle for a period called DIFS. Then count down a random backoff, one idle slot at a time. If the channel goes busy, freeze the counter. Do not reset it. Transmit when it reaches zero. Then the receiver checks the frame, and after a shorter gap called SIFS, returns an ACK. Why shorter? So the ACK goes first, before anyone else can grab the channel. If no ACK arrives, the sender retries with a larger backoff range.

## 14.4.counter
Trace a counter. It starts at 4. Two idle slots go by, and it reads 2. Another station transmits for a long time, and the counter stays at 2. When the channel is idle again, two more slots bring it to zero, and we can send. The waiting already done is not thrown away, which makes things fairer for stations that have waited.

## 14.4.noack
And a missing ACK does not prove a collision. The data might have been corrupted by noise, or the ACK itself might have been lost. Note too that this is a local, link-layer ACK, not TCP's. It repairs one radio hop. Together with coding, retries, and rate changes, that's wireless error recovery. It's done locally because the radio hop is where the errors happen. Local repair doesn't guarantee end to end delivery, though.

## 14.4.rts
CSMA/CA still doesn't cure the hidden terminal. For that, there's an optional reservation. Before sending data, A sends a short request to send, RTS, to B. B answers with clear to send, CTS. Here's the clever part. CTS comes from the receiver, so everybody near the receiver hears it, including hidden C. Now C knows to stay quiet. Then A sends the data, and B acknowledges.

## 14.4.nav
How long should C stay quiet? The CTS carries a duration, and C sets a timer, called the network allocation vector, or NAV. This acts as virtual carrier sensing. Even during silent gaps between the protected frames, C treats the channel as busy.

## 14.4.tradeoff
Is this free? No. RTS frames can themselves collide, but they're short, so a collision costs much less than losing a long data frame. And the four-frame exchange adds overhead. For short frames on a quiet channel, that overhead can exceed what it saves. So choose it for long frames under real contention.

## 14.4.power
Radio also costs battery, and listening is expensive. In power management, a station tells its AP it's going to sleep. The AP buffers any frames for it. Each beacon lists who has data waiting. So the station wakes just in time for the beacon, and if it's named, it asks for its frames. Otherwise it goes straight back to sleep.

## 14.4.idlelisten
Notice the shared pattern with soft state and with queues. The AP holds data on the sleeper's behalf, and a periodic signal tells it when to check. You pay with delay, and you gain battery. There's a tradeoff in every design here.

## 14.4.bluetooth
Bluetooth shows yet another coordination model. A piconet has one controller, which schedules its nearby devices, so there's no contention at all. It also uses frequency hopping, moving among channels in a pattern. The slide's classic figures are 79 channels, and a 625 microsecond slot. Those describe the classic system, not all Bluetooth modes.

## 14.4.bluetooth-more
With 625 microseconds per slot, 1,600 slots fit in one second. The controller grants one client a transmit opportunity at a time, and the group follows a shared hopping sequence. These are classic Bluetooth's numbers, so don't apply them to every mode.

## 14.4.pitfall
A hidden terminal isn't simply a weak signal. The key is two senders that can't hear each other, while both reach a shared receiver.

## 14.4.changed
Test yourself on the geometry. A sends RTS to B. B's CTS is heard by C, which can't hear A. Separately, E can hear A, but not B.

## 14.4.predict
Which station sets its NAV from the CTS? Which station hears only the RTS? And what might each one do?

## 14.4.answer
C hears the CTS, so it sets its NAV, and it defers. That protects B's reception. E hears only the RTS. RTS frames also carry a duration, so in the standard behavior, E sets its NAV too, even though it might not harm B. E is exposed, and the shortcut of ignoring it is the idealization we flagged. So the radio link now works, for a laptop that stays put. But what if it doesn't? When a phone drives across a city, changing towers every few minutes, how can a call stay up, and how can anyone even find it?
