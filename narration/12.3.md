# Dish 12.3: Inside an AS

## 12.3.question
We've been drawing each AS as a dot. But a large provider has hundreds of routers across a continent, and it may hear about the same destination at several different border routers. How do all its internal routers learn about that, and how does each one pick an exit?

## 12.3.ibgp
A BGP session between different ASes is called eBGP, for external. A session between routers within the same AS is iBGP, for internal. Here's the flow. Border router R1 learns a route over eBGP. It passes this along to R2 and R3 over iBGP. Now all of them know the route. Note that internal routers don't add their AS number again as the update moves around inside.

## 12.3.igp
And iBGP does not replace the internal routing protocol. It says that a destination is reachable through a certain exit. But it doesn't say how to get across your own network to that exit. That's still the job of OSPF or IS-IS. BGP and the IGP solve two halves of one problem.

## 12.3.nexthop
How those halves are joined. BGP says: 203.0.113.0/24 has next hop 192.0.2.9. But that router may be several hops away. So the router looks up 192.0.2.9 in its internal routes, and finds it goes through neighbor 198.51.100.2 on port 3. It combines the two, and installs: 203.0.113.0/24 goes through 198.51.100.2 on port 3. A BGP next hop isn't necessarily a directly connected neighbor. It has to be resolved first.

## 12.3.order
Now the decision process can be stated in full, in the lecture's simplified order. Highest local preference. Then shortest AS path. Then lowest internal cost to the exit. Then a tie break. Compare in order, and stop as soon as one route remains. These are not added into a score. There's no formula that combines them.

## 12.3.decide
Try three routes for one prefix. A has local preference 140, length 4, and exit cost 50. B has 100, length 2, and cost 5. C has 100, length 2, and cost 1. With all three available, A wins immediately on preference, despite being the worst by every other number. Withdraw A. B and C tie on preference, and tie on length, so internal cost decides: C wins. Now shorten B's path to length 1. B beats C at step two, and its higher exit cost never gets compared.

## 12.3.hot
That third step has a nickname: hot-potato routing. When outside attributes tie, hand the traffic off at the nearest exit, and spend as little of your own network as possible. Picture a network with exits on the west and east coasts. A router with internal costs 4 and 9 picks west. A router on the other side, with costs 12 and 3, picks east. Each one minimizes its own carrying cost. And so the return path, chosen by a different network with its own hot potatoes, may take a different route. Internet paths are often asymmetric for just this reason.

## 12.3.default
Does every router need all those routes? No. A customer with a single provider has only one way out. It can use a default route: a slash 0, matching everything, pointing at the provider. Any longer prefix beats it. So a stub network, or an internal router at the edge, can hold a very small table. Full tables belong where real choices exist, not everywhere.

## 12.3.pitfall
The classic confusion. A BGP next hop isn't necessarily the neighbor you hand the frame to. It can be several routers away, so it has to be resolved through internal routing.

## 12.3.changed
Change the costs. Internal maintenance raises router R's cost to the west exit from 4 to 11. Its east cost stays at 9. Nothing outside the AS has changed.

## 12.3.predict
Does R's exit change? And does some neighbor network now see traffic it wasn't seeing before?

## 12.3.answer
Yes. R now prefers east, at 9. Its traffic moves to a different exit, and possibly to a different neighbor network. An internal change shifted traffic outside the AS. That's the coupling between BGP and the internal protocol. We've now covered how the decision works. But why do networks set these preferences in the first place? Because each link between two networks has a contract, and someone pays.
