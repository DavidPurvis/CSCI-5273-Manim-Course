# Dish 15.1: The cellular core

## 15.1.question
Our laptop has become a phone, and now it's moving at highway speed, while it streams video. Its local Wi-Fi tricks are no help at this scale. A cellular carrier must find the phone anywhere in the country, keep its session alive across towers, and bill the right person. What kind of network can do all this?

## 15.1.g2
Follow the history, since it explains the design. 2G was built for voice, like the telephone network. A radio site, called a BTS, connects to a base station controller, a BSC. Those connect to a mobile switching center, the MSC, and a gateway leads to the public telephone network. It's circuit switched throughout.

## 15.1.g3
3G had to add data, without breaking voice. So it built a parallel packet core. The SGSN and GGSN form a data path to the Internet, sitting right beside the unchanged voice path. Voice and data share the radio side, and split at the edge of the core. That's the key insight: coexistence, not replacement.

## 15.1.g3-more
Watch one handset use both. Its voice call follows the MSC side. Its packet data follows the SGSN and GGSN side, toward the Internet.

## 15.1.g4
4G LTE makes a clean break. Everything, even voice, is carried as IP packets through one all-IP core. There's no separate circuit side. But all-IP doesn't mean ordinary routers and nothing else. LTE keeps specialized machinery for subscribers, authentication, location, radio scheduling, and tunnels.

## 15.1.ue
Let's assign each piece one main role. The UE, or user element, is the device, along with its subscription. The subscription is identified by an IMSI. This is an identity that stays the same, not a location. The slide calls it 64 bit, and draws it like a MAC address. In the standard, it's a string of no more than 15 decimal digits. The point to keep is that it's separate from any MAC, IP, or tunnel address.

## 15.1.enb
The eNodeB is the base station. It's not a passive relay. It manages radio resources, and takes an active part in attachment and handover. It's where the wireless hop meets the core.

## 15.1.enb-more
For example, base station A decides that a target, B, should serve a moving phone. It asks B for resources, and later directs the phone there. The core's tunnel state then moves to match. An ordinary Ethernet switch does nothing like this.

## 15.1.hss
The home subscriber server, the HSS, lives in the subscriber's home network. It stores subscriber profiles and authentication keys, and it cooperates in authentication. It isn't on the path of your data at all.

## 15.1.hss-more
Picture a phone appearing in a visited network. Its mobility manager coordinates with the home HSS, to authenticate the subscription and set up state for the visit. That's a database and control role. No user packet ever passes through it.

## 15.1.mme
The mobility management entity, the MME, is the coordinator. It handles authentication with the HSS, tracks where the device is, handles paging, and sets up the tunnels. It tells other boxes what to do, but user data doesn't pass through it.

## 15.1.gw
The serving gateway and the packet gateway are on the user's data path. The P-GW connects to external networks, such as the Internet. The S-GW joins the radio-side tunnel to the tunnel toward the P-GW. To see the point, watch a packet. It goes UE, eNodeB, S-GW, P-GW. It never touches the MME or the HSS.

## 15.1.pgw-more
The P-GW is the cellular system's outward doorway. The radio cell can change, while traffic stays anchored through that gateway. It may also perform NAT. But NAT translation and tunnel encapsulation are different operations, so don't treat them as one.

## 15.1.rule
Draw two separate pictures. The control story has the HSS and the MME. The payload story has the S-GW and the P-GW. A control message can change a data path without its sender being one of the hops. That's the data plane and control plane split again, from chapter 3.

## 15.1.pitfall
A tempting error is to route user data through every box in the diagram. The HSS and MME do control work. Payload travels through the base station, the S-GW, and the P-GW.

## 15.1.changed
Test that. Suppose the MME crashes for a moment, while a phone is sitting still, in the middle of a video stream. Separately, suppose the S-GW crashes.

## 15.1.predict
Which failure immediately stops the video's packets? And which one affects moving phones, and new ones?

## 15.1.answer
An S-GW failure stops the packets right away, since it's on the data path. The tunnels that are already set up don't pass through the MME, so its failure hits control tasks first, like attaching, paging, and handover. So, the data flows through a chain of tunnels.
